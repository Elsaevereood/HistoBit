Alexander the Great fought for 15 years across 22,000 miles of territory. He never lost a battle. Military historians have spent two thousand years analyzing his tactics, his leadership, his psychological grip on his men.

Almost none of them spend much time on what actually made it possible: the fact that his army always had food, water, and equipment.

## The Problem Every Ancient Army Faced

Ancient armies moved at the speed of their supply. A force of 40,000 men needs roughly 80,000 pounds of grain per day, plus water, plus fodder for horses and pack animals. In the ancient world, this either came with you or you found it along the route.

Most ancient commanders solved this problem badly. They stripped the land, created famine, alienated local populations, and eventually found themselves marching through hostile territory with empty supply trains. Attrition did the rest.

Alexander solved it differently.

## What Alexander Actually Did

**He traveled lighter than anyone thought possible.** Before the Persian campaign, Alexander banned wagons for officers and soldiers. Commanders who had previously traveled with enormous baggage trains — furniture, servants, luxuries — were stripped down to the essentials. The army moved faster because it carried less.

**He planned routes around supply, not around tactics.** The Persian campaign route was not chosen for military convenience. It was chosen because it followed the Persian Royal Road, which had established supply depots, fresh water at regular intervals, and local populations capable of provisioning an army. Alexander used the Persian infrastructure he had just conquered.

**He never fought a battle he couldn't afford to win slowly.** This sounds obvious. It wasn't. Most ancient commanders sought decisive engagement and assumed victory would solve the supply problem. Alexander ensured supply security first, then sought battle. He never needed a quick victory because he could afford to wait.

**He built ports and supply depots as he advanced.** Along the Mediterranean coast, Alexander systematically captured or built harbors, establishing a naval supply line that could follow the army. When he moved inland, he pre-positioned supplies through local administrators and satraps who had been brought under Macedonian control.

## The Gedrosian Disaster

Alexander was not infallible. His one catastrophic logistical failure — the march through the Gedrosian Desert in 325 BC — demonstrates exactly how essential his supply discipline was, because when it broke down, the results were horrific.

Returning from India, Alexander chose to march through the Makran desert in modern-day Pakistan and Iran. The reasons are debated: punishment of a rebellious region, a desire to outdo previous heroes, strategic miscalculation.

The army had no pre-positioned supplies. There was almost no water. The heat was lethal. Flash floods destroyed what little food they carried. Historians estimate the army lost a quarter of its men — some say more — to thirst, starvation, and exhaustion. It was the worst march of Alexander's career and it was entirely self-inflicted.

The contrast with his normal campaigns is stark. When Alexander controlled his logistics, he was undefeatable. When he didn't, men died in thousands without a battle fought.

## The Commander's Real Job

There is a tendency to attribute great military commanders' success to their tactical genius, their personal charisma, their ability to inspire men to die for them. These things matter. They are not the foundation.

The foundation is whether the men are fed, whether the weapons are maintained, whether the routes are known, whether the supply trains are protected.

Alexander understood this at 20 years old. He reorganized the Macedonian army's logistics before he ever crossed into Asia. He abolished the wagon trains not because he was ascetic but because he understood that speed and supply security were more valuable than comfort.

The battles are what people remember. The planning before the battles is what made the victories possible.

Every general who has failed — and the history of warfare is mostly a history of failure — failed because they assumed the logistics would work out. Alexander never assumed. He ensured.
