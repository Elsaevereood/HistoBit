Everyone who tells the story of 15 September 1916 reaches for the same number. Forty-nine tanks were meant to go forward at **Flers-Courcelette**. Most of them never got where they were going. They threw tracks, sank into shell holes, seized up on the approach march, or simply stopped. By the time the infantry went over the top, the British Army's secret weapon had mostly disqualified itself.

It is a satisfying story. It confirms what people already believe about the Somme: that the men at the top were careless with lives and careless with tools, that a war-winning invention was squandered because a field marshal could not wait. **Winston Churchill** thought so. So did **Ernest Swinton**, who had helped design the thing. Both of them said so in print, and both of them were right about one particular thing and wrong about almost everything else.

Because the number proves nothing. The men who wrote the operations orders for 15 September had already assumed the tanks would break down. They put it in writing. They told the infantry, in advance and in plain language, not to wait.

## The Objection Was About Surprise, Not Performance

Churchill's objection came first and came early. In a memorandum dated 7 January 1916, while the machines were still a secret being built under the cover story of water carriers for Mesopotamia, he warned that none should be used until all could be used at once. Swinton, a lieutenant-colonel and one of the earliest advocates for the whole concept, had made the same argument in June 1915. There should be no preliminary efforts with a few machines, he wrote. Show the enemy one, and you have shown him everything.

Their logic was sound and their conclusion was about surprise, not about combat performance. Surprise is a resource you spend once. Fifty tanks on a narrow front in September could not do what five hundred tanks on a broad front might do the following spring, and once the Germans had seen the machines they would start building the guns and the ammunition to kill them.

<FactBox>
Which they did, and quickly. Armour-piercing rounds fired from Mauser rifles would stop a Mark I. German gunners at Flers worked that out inside a day. The secret was worth exactly one morning, and that is the honest cost of 15 September 1916.
</FactBox>

## How a Surprise Argument Became a Performance Verdict

**J.F.C. Fuller**, writing after the war in *Tanks in the Great War*, turned the surprise argument into a performance verdict, and the performance verdict stuck. The Canadian official history was blunter still, concluding that the armour in its first action failed to carry out the tasks assigned to it.

That sentence is the origin of the modern myth, and the trouble with it is the last four words. Nobody who reads the orders written before the battle can honestly claim the tanks failed the tasks assigned to them, because the tasks assigned to them were small.

This is how most military myths are built. Not by liars, but by later writers borrowing a conclusion from an argument that was about something else, exactly the way [the Hessians got written into American memory as mercenaries](/blog/hessians-not-mercenaries) on the strength of a propaganda label nobody checked.

## Read the Paperwork

The 12th Battalion of the East Surrey Regiment received an operations order defining the tanks' job as destroying machine guns and strongpoints and clearing the way. The same document instructed that if the tanks went out of action, the infantry were on no account to wait for them, because waiting meant losing the artillery barrage, and losing the barrage meant dying.

On the Canadian side the 27th Battalion put it even more flatly. Two tanks would probably work with them, the order noted, but plans were to be made irrespective of them.

That is not a commander expecting a breakthrough weapon. That is a commander hedging against a machine he does not trust, and hedging correctly. It is the same distinction that separates a system doing its job badly from a system nobody had actually asked to do the job, which is the whole argument over [the forty-three minutes at Pearl Harbor](/blog/pearl-harbor-system-failure).

## Forty-Nine Machines, Four Corps

Forty-nine tanks of C and D Companies, Heavy Section, Machine Gun Corps went into the operation, drawn from around 150 then in existence.

| Formation | Tanks allotted |
| --- | --- |
| XIV Corps | 17 |
| XV Corps | 17 |
| III Corps | 8 |
| Reserve Army (Canadian Corps) | 7 |

Zero hour was 0620. The single aggregate figures you find quoted everywhere for how many actually fought contradict each other badly, because different sources counted at different hours and different points on the road, so the honest way to measure the day is corps by corps. Do it that way and the picture sharpens considerably.

## Courcelette: Four Failed, Two Fought

Start with the Canadians. Six tanks crossed with the 2nd Canadian Division, split between the 4th and 6th Infantry Brigades for a converging attack on a fortified sugar beet refinery the men called the **Sugar Factory**. Four of the six never made it past the Canadian front line.

C.3, *Chartreuse*, took shrapnel in its rear steering gear and drove into a shell hole it could not climb out of. C.4, *Chablis*, threw its tracks. C.1, *Champagne*, bogged down with its tracks spinning uselessly in the mud, and the crew eventually walked away from it. C.2, *Cognac*, disappeared into a crater. Troops were detached to try to free the ditched machines. The 28th Battalion's war diary recorded that the attempt was futile.

Two tanks were left. What those two did is the whole argument.

<StatBox number="2" label="tanks still running in front of Courcelette out of the six that started, and the two that decided how the Canadian attack ended" />

Private **Donald Fraser** of the 31st Battalion was out in no man's land with the 6th Brigade when the attack came apart. German machine gun and rifle fire pinned the advance flat. Platoon commanders who lifted themselves above the lip of a crater to lead their men forward were shot down as they rose. Fraser wrote afterwards that the assault was a failure and that the men were at the mercy of the enemy.

Then a shape came grinding through the smoke behind them.

It was C.5, *Crème de Menthe*, a male Mark I carrying two naval six-pounders and four Vickers guns, commanded by Captain **Arthur Inglis**, an infantry officer seconded from the Gloucestershire Regiment. It opened fire on the German positions. In Fraser's account the tank gave new life and vigour to the men, who got up off the ground and followed it forward as if to be in at the kill. They took the trench. Private Joseph Thompson wrote that his comrades thought crushing the German positions was such quick work that they asked permission to push on ahead, and were given it.

Over on the 4th Brigade front, Second Lieutenant **John Allan** brought C.6, *Cordon Rouge*, astride a German trench and poured enfilade fire down its length in both directions.

## Flers: The Tank in the High Street

The pattern repeated to the east. Four tanks were assigned to the New Zealand Division and two survived to fight. The 3rd Battalion of the New Zealand Rifle Brigade ran into uncut wire and heavy fire at Flers Trench and stopped, unable to advance, and waited.

At about 1030 two machines arrived, Lieutenant **Herbert Pearsall's** D.11 and Captain **Graeme Nixon's** D.12. D.11 covered the flank. D.12 went at the wire and the machine guns. The New Zealanders took Flers Trench and pushed on to Abbey Road Trench by 1100, an advance of roughly 2,230 metres, one of the deepest made by any division that day.

And in the village of Flers itself, Second Lieutenant **Stuart Hastie's** D.17, *Dinnaken*, worked up the main street with men of the 122nd Brigade behind it. The war diary of the 15th Hampshires, still groping for a word to describe a thing nobody had a word for, called it an armoured car, and recorded that it did most useful work smashing enemy strongpoints and gave the men great confidence. Two days later the *Daily Chronicle* printed an airman's wireless report of a tank walking up the high street of Flers with the British Army cheering behind. Hastie got the Military Cross.

## The First Tank in Action Anywhere in the World

It had gone in over an hour earlier. At 0515 Captain **Harold Mortimore** took D.1, *Daredevil*, across no man's land toward Delville Wood, ahead of the general advance. He was 23, he had volunteered for the tanks after crashing an aeroplane in training with the Royal Naval Air Service, and he had never been in a battle.

He got his machine astride a German trench and opened fire. He remembered the faces of the Germans coming up out of their dugouts for the rest of his life. Shortly afterwards *Daredevil* was knocked out, its steering hit. The Tank Museum records the fire as German artillery. Mortimore's daughter, Dr Tilly Mortimore, has said it was British. The crew got out. The tank stayed on the field.

## What the Day Actually Cost

None of this made the day a victory in the way **Haig** wanted. The cavalry never got their gap. The deepest first-day objective, Gueudecourt, would not fall until 26 September. The cost was brutal in the specific, unglamorous way the Somme was always brutal.

## A Useful Accessory, and Nothing More

Lieutenant-General Sir **Julian Byng**, commanding the Canadian Corps, wrote the verdict that the historian Andrew McEwen would later borrow for the title of the best study of the day's fighting.

<PullQuote text="Tanks are a useful accessory to the infantry, but nothing more." attribution="Lt-Gen Sir Julian Byng, commanding the Canadian Corps" />

He was right, and he was not insulting them. That was the job.

Haig came away pleased and overstated it, claiming that wherever the tanks advanced the British took their objectives, which is not quite true, since the 47th Division took High Wood without a tank anywhere near them. Within two days he was asking London for as many more as they could send. He eventually ordered a thousand. Swinton, still bitter about the lost surprise years later, nonetheless wrote that the machines had fully justified themselves.

---
## The Verdict

What happened on 15 September 1916 was not a weapon failing. It was a weapon doing a small job, in small numbers, on ground that would have stopped anything mechanical, for men who had been told not to count on it and did not.

Judge it against the breakthrough it never promised and it looks like a fiasco. Judge it against the orders actually written, and against Donald Fraser lying in a shell hole watching a machine nobody had a name for come out of the smoke and put his platoon back on its feet, and it looks like something else entirely.

The tanks were never asked to win the Somme. They were asked to get the infantry across sixty yards of open ground. Twice that morning, in front of Courcelette, they did.
