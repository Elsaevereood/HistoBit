The fisherman had stopped counting crossings somewhere around midnight. His palms had split hours ago, the salt water finding every crack as he hauled the muffled oars through another stroke, another, another. No one in the boat spoke. Orders. Behind him sat maybe forty soldiers packed shoulder to shoulder, muskets upright between their knees, the gunwale riding so low that black river water sat inches from spilling in. Somewhere out in the dark, invisible, dozens of other boats were doing the same thing. The far shore existed only as a memory. Fog had swallowed it.

He was a **Marblehead** man, one of Colonel **John Glover**'s Massachusetts regiment, fishermen and merchant sailors who had spent their lives reading water like other men read scripture. Tonight that skill was the only thing standing between an army and its grave. Because behind him, on the Brooklyn shore he kept rowing away from, campfires still burned in the American lines. The fires were a lie. The trenches behind them were emptying, man by man, in silence, and if the enemy six hundred yards away noticed before dawn, everything ended. Not the battle. Everything.

---
## The Largest Force Britain Had Ever Sent Anywhere

Two months earlier, in July 1776, the largest expeditionary force **Britain** had ever sent anywhere in the world began gathering in **New York**'s lower harbor. Forty-five ships appeared off **Staten Island** in a matter of hours, and they kept coming until more than 400 vessels crowded the water and some 32,000 professional soldiers, including around 8,000 hired **Hessians**, stood ready to land. Those German troops were not the mercenaries American propaganda made them, which is [a myth worth taking apart on its own](/blog/hessians-not-mercenaries). One American rifleman wrote that the masts looked like a forest of trimmed pine trees. London had decided to end the rebellion in one season, and General **William Howe**, stinging from the humiliation of being chased out of Boston in March, had picked the perfect place to do it. New York had one of the finest harbors on earth, a Loyalist population happy to see the King's men, and the **Hudson River** running like a blade into the heart of the colonies.

**George Washington** understood all of this. He also understood, as General Charles Lee had warned, that whoever commanded the sea commanded the town, and the **Royal Navy** commanded the sea absolutely. Congress wanted New York held anyway. So Washington took roughly 19,000 men, most of them farmers and clerks who had never stood under artillery fire, and split them between **Manhattan** and a ring of forts on **Brooklyn Heights** across the **East River**. On July 2 he told them what they were standing on: "The fate of unborn millions will now depend, under God, on the courage and conduct of this army."

Two days later, Congress declared independence. The men cheered, pulled down the statue of **George III** on the Bowling Green, and melted it into 42,000 musket balls. Then they went back to digging trenches and waiting for the hammer.

---
## Five Men Guarding the Door

It fell on August 22. Some 15,000 British and Hessian troops came ashore at **Gravesend Bay** on Long Island, unopposed, with more following until 20,000 stood on the island. Washington, convinced the landing might be a feint before a strike at Manhattan, reinforced Brooklyn only partially. Worse, his command there was a mess. **Nathanael Greene**, the one general who knew the Brooklyn ground intimately, went down with fever. John Sullivan replaced him, then Israel Putnam replaced Sullivan, each less familiar with the terrain than the last.

The American defense hinged on the **Heights of Guan**, a wooded ridge running across the British path to Brooklyn. Four passes cut through it. The Americans put 550 men on the Gowanus Road by the bay, 1,100 at Flatbush Pass, 800 at Bedford Pass. The fourth gap, **Jamaica Pass**, sat three miles out on the far left, and Putnam did not believe the British even knew it existed. He posted five militia officers there. Five men, on horseback, watching the door to the entire position.

The British knew it existed. Local Loyalists told General **Henry Clinton** about the pass and offered to guide him through it, and Clinton built the whole battle plan around that one piece of intelligence. General James Grant would demonstrate noisily against the American right near the bay. The Hessians under Leopold von Heister would press the center at Flatbush. And Clinton himself would lead 10,000 men on a nine-mile night march around the American left, through Jamaica Pass, into the rear of every man on the ridge.

---
## The Trap Closes

The column moved out at nine in the evening on August 26, guns wrapped, no talking. Around two in the morning it reached Howard's Tavern near the pass, where Howe personally gave the tavern keeper William Howard a choice his son remembered for the rest of his life: guide the army, or, in Howe's words, "I shall shoot you through the head." Howard guided them. Minutes later British dragoons scooped up the five American officers watching the pass, who surrendered without firing a shot because they thought the approaching horsemen were American. Clinton questioned them and learned the thing he could barely believe. They were the only guards. The door was open.

At nine the next morning, two British signal guns fired near Bedford, and the trap closed. Sullivan's men in the center suddenly had Hessian bayonets in front of them and 10,000 British regulars behind them. The line did not bend. It disintegrated. Men ran through cornfields and woods toward the Brooklyn forts with Hessians hunting them through the trees, and there were persistent American accounts of surrendering men bayoneted where they stood. Sullivan himself was dragged out of a cornfield as a prisoner.

## Four Hundred Men Against Two Thousand

Only the American right still held its shape. There, near the bay, General William Alexander, an American who styled himself **Lord Stirling**, had been standing toe to toe with Grant's diversionary force since dawn, his **Maryland** and **Delaware** regiments trading disciplined volleys with British regulars for four hours. These were some of the only troops on the field with real training, and they were proving it. Then Stirling looked behind him and understood the morning. **Cornwallis**'s grenadiers had come down from the captured passes and were sitting across his line of retreat, anchored on a Dutch stone farmhouse, the **Vechte-Cortelyou house**, with two cannon. His division was in a closing fist.

Stirling made the kind of decision that gets remembered for 250 years. He ordered most of his men to escape as best they could across the **Gowanus marsh**, eighty yards of tidal creek and sucking mud. And to buy them the time to do it, he personally led about 400 Marylanders under Major **Mordecai Gist** straight at the stone house and the 2,000 British around it.

![Maryland Continental soldiers advancing on the Vechte-Cortelyou stone house during the Battle of Brooklyn, August 27, 1776](/images/blog/brooklyn-1776-maryland-400.jpg)

They charged. Grapeshot and massed musketry tore the ranks apart, and the survivors fell back, reformed, and charged again. Accounts disagree on whether they went in twice or six times. The men inside it were not counting. Corporal William McMillan, twenty years old, remembered it plainly: "My Captain was killed, first lieutenant was killed, second lieutenant shot through the hand, two sergeants were killed, one in front of me." His bayonet was shot off his musket. Eighty percent of his company was killed or captured.

Washington watched it all from a redoubt on Cobble Hill, close enough to see the lines shred and reform. The Maryland Gazette reported two weeks later that he wrung his hands and cried out, "Good God, what brave fellows I must this day lose!" The battalion's loss came to 256 men killed, wounded and captured, a figure later monuments hardened into 256 dead. Whatever the true split, the unit was gutted. Gist and a handful of others cut their way out through the marsh, where retreating men were drowning under their packs. Stirling refused to hand his sword to the British and instead fought through to von Heister's Hessians and surrendered to them, a last gesture of contempt. But the charges had held Cornwallis in place, and behind that shield, the wreck of the American right escaped into the Brooklyn forts.

## The Most Expensive Caution in British History

By afternoon, Howe had won the battle completely. Around 300 Americans were dead, some 800 wounded, and more than 1,000 captured, most of them destined for the rotting prison ships where far more Americans would die than in any battle of the war. British losses ran to about 400. Some 9,000 shaken American troops were now penned inside the Brooklyn lines with the East River at their backs, and the Royal Navy needed only a fair wind to sail into that river and lock the cage. Howe's officers begged to storm the works that evening. Howe, who had watched the slaughter of his own men storming **Bunker Hill** a year earlier, said no. He would take the position by siege, cheaply, in a few days. It was the most expensive act of caution in British history.

Then the weather turned. A nor'easter blew in on August 28, and for two days rain fell in sheets, filling the trenches, soaking every cartridge in the American lines until whole regiments could not have fired a volley to save their lives. But the same wind pinned the Royal Navy out of the East River. Washington, who had barely slept since the battle, read the situation coldly. General **Thomas Mifflin** inspected the perimeter and found British siege trenches creeping to within 600 yards. On the afternoon of August 29 the council of war voted unanimously: evacuate that night. Mifflin volunteered his Pennsylvanians for the death watch, the rear guard that would hold the empty lines and be the last men out, if they got out at all.

---
## Thirteen Hours in the Dark

What followed was the finest thing Washington ever did as a soldier, and it involved no fighting whatsoever. Every flat-bottomed boat and sloop on the surrounding waters had been quietly collected. The troops were told they were being readied for a night attack, because the truth would have caused a stampede. At the ferry landing, Glover's Marbleheaders took the oars. Wagon wheels were wrapped in rags. Talking was forbidden. The campfires stayed lit and the rear guard walked the parapets, making an army out of a few regiments.

It nearly failed twice. The wind and current were so violent early on that the boats could not cross at all, and for hours the plan simply hung there, dead in the water, until around eleven the wind dropped. Then, deep in the night, an aide named Alexander Scammell delivered a garbled order and Mifflin's entire covering force abandoned the front lines hours early. Washington met them near the ferry, aghast. "Good God, General Mifflin," he said, "I am afraid you have ruined us." The rear guard turned around and marched back into the empty trenches, and the British, 600 yards away, never noticed the American line had ceased to exist for the better part of an hour.

Dawn came, and the army was still crossing. Hundreds of men, Washington among them, stood exposed on the Brooklyn shore as the sky lightened, waiting for the first British sentry to look down at the ferry landing and understand. And then the fog came. A dense, drifting fog rolled off the water and buried the shoreline so completely that men could barely see across a boat's length, and it held, hour after hour, while the last regiments embarked. Washington stepped into one of the final boats. Around seven in the morning the fog lifted, and British troops pushed into the American works to find fires still smoldering, trenches empty, and the last boats fading toward Manhattan out of musket range.

## An Escape, Not a Victory

Strip away the fog and the folklore and look at what actually happened in those nine days. The United States lost its first major battle as an independent nation, lost it about as badly as a battle can be lost, and lost New York City for the next seven years. Every material measure of the campaign belongs to Howe. But wars between empires and insurgencies are not scored by material measures. Howe needed to destroy the **Continental Army**. Washington only needed the army to exist on September 1, and it did. The men who crossed the East River that night crossed the **Delaware** four months later.

The Revolution was not saved at Trenton or Saratoga or Yorktown. It was saved first, in the dark, by fishermen rowing in silence, by 400 Marylanders dying in front of a farmhouse, and by a commander who understood that the only battle he could not afford to lose was the argument for tomorrow. America's founding military achievement is not a victory. It is an escape.
