In the summer of 216 BC, the Roman Republic sent its largest army ever assembled into the field. Roughly 86,000 men — consuls, legions, allied troops — marched south to crush Hannibal Barca once and for all. By sunset, 70,000 of them were dead.

## The Setup

Hannibal had been in Italy for two years. He had already beaten Rome twice — at the Trebia River and Lake Trasimene — using the same basic trick each time: draw the Romans forward, hit them from the sides. The Romans knew this. They appointed a dictator, Fabius Maximus, who refused to fight Hannibal directly and slowly starved his army by cutting supply lines.

It worked. Rome was winning without fighting.

Then Fabius's term ended. The Roman people were furious at what they saw as cowardice. They elected two new consuls — Gaius Terentius Varro and Lucius Aemilius Paullus — with a mandate to end the war in one decisive battle. They got their battle.

## The Trap

Hannibal chose the battlefield at Cannae, a flat plain near the Aufidus River. Flat ground suited Roman tactics — tight formation, push forward, win through mass and discipline. Hannibal knew this. He chose flat ground deliberately.

He arranged his army in a convex arc, with his weakest infantry — Gauls and Spanish — bulging forward in the center, and his strongest troops, the African veterans, held back on the flanks. His cavalry, numerically superior, was placed on both wings.

The Romans formed up opposite. Varro commanded. He packed his legions tighter than normal — deeper formation, less width — trying to punch through Hannibal's center with overwhelming mass.

This was exactly what Hannibal wanted.

## The Double Envelopment

When the battle began, the Roman cavalry routed Hannibal's weaker cavalry on one wing almost immediately. Standard Roman cavalry performance. On the other wing, Hannibal's superior Numidian cavalry pinned and tied down the Roman allied horse.

In the center, the Roman legions slammed into Hannibal's Gauls and Spanish. The Carthaginian center began to bend — then deliberately gave ground, retreating slowly, pulling the Roman mass forward. The convex arc became concave. The Romans, sensing breakthrough, pressed harder, packing themselves tighter, pushing deeper into what was now a pocket.

The African veterans on the flanks had not moved. Now they wheeled inward.

The Roman cavalry that had routed the enemy horse came back around and sealed the rear.

86,000 men were now surrounded on all sides by a smaller army.

## The Killing

What followed was not a battle. It was a contained slaughter that lasted hours. The Romans were packed so tightly they could barely raise their weapons. Men suffocated. Men were stabbed from every direction. There was nowhere to run.

Polybius records 70,000 Roman dead. Modern historians debate the number but not the scale. Among the dead: the consul Paullus, 80 Roman senators, and roughly a third of Rome's officer class.

Hannibal lost roughly 6,000 men.

## Why It Didn't End the War

Cannae should have ended Rome. It didn't for one reason: Rome refused to negotiate. The Senate met the next day and passed resolutions forbidding any discussion of peace. They raised new legions, enslaved 8,000 men to fill the ranks, and kept fighting.

Hannibal had destroyed Rome's army. He had not destroyed Rome's will.

He would spend 13 more years in Italy, winning battles, never winning the war. Scipio Africanus would eventually take the fight to Carthage itself, and Hannibal would be recalled to defend his homeland. At Zama in 202 BC, Scipio used the same double envelopment Hannibal had used at Cannae.

The student had learned from the master.
