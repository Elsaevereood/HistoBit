Every textbook gives you the same story. Napoleon invaded Russia. Winter came. The army froze. Napoleon retreated. The end.

This is wrong. The army was already dying in summer.

## The Numbers Tell the Real Story

Napoleon crossed the Niemen River with approximately 685,000 men in June 1812. By the time he reached Moscow in September, he had roughly 100,000 combat-effective troops. Moscow had not yet fallen. The winter had not yet arrived.

He lost 585,000 men before the cold became a factor. The question is how.

## The Supply Problem

The Grande Armée was the largest army ever assembled in European history. Feeding it was a logistical problem no military organization of that era could actually solve.

Napoleon's supply system relied on a combination of pre-positioned magazines and living off the land. In western Europe, this had worked because the land was rich, the population was dense, and the campaign seasons were short. Russia was different in every way.

Russia is vast. The distances are immense. The population in the western borderlands was sparse. The Russian military, under Barclay de Tolly and then Kutuzov, deliberately retreated, stripping the land as they went — burning crops, driving off livestock, destroying wells. What the Russian army didn't destroy, Russian peasants did.

The Grande Armée marched through a scorched corridor with 685,000 mouths to feed and almost nothing to eat.

## What Killed the Army

**Dysentery and typhus killed more men than any battle.** Contaminated water sources, spoiled food, men eating anything they could find — including dead horses — produced epidemic disease within weeks of crossing the border. By July, regiments that had started with 3,000 men were down to 1,500 without a single major engagement.

**The horses died first.** The army's cavalry and artillery depended on roughly 150,000 horses. Horses need enormous quantities of forage daily. There was no forage. Horses began dying in July. By the time the army reached Smolensk, the artillery was critically undermanned because there were no horses to pull guns. This degraded Napoleon's ability to fight decisive battles and finish engagements quickly — every battle became more costly because he couldn't pursue and destroy retreating Russian forces.

**The heat was lethal.** June and July 1812 saw unusually high temperatures in Russia. Men in full uniform, marching 20-30 miles a day, collapsed from heat exhaustion in enormous numbers. Water was scarce and often contaminated. The army that crossed the Niemen was not the army that reached Borodino.

## Borodino

On September 7, Napoleon finally caught Kutuzov and forced a battle at Borodino, 70 miles west of Moscow. The Russians fought in fixed positions. The French attacked repeatedly. By nightfall, roughly 70,000 men on both sides were casualties.

Napoleon had roughly 130,000 men going in. He committed most of them. He did not commit his Imperial Guard — 20,000 of his best troops — when it might have broken the Russian line completely. He was ill. He was cautious. He was not the Napoleon of Austerlitz.

Kutuzov retreated. Napoleon had won the field and changed nothing.

## Moscow

Napoleon entered Moscow expecting to negotiate peace. The city was burning — the Russians had set it on fire. He waited for 35 days for a Russian delegation that never came. Tsar Alexander, pressured by his court not to give in, simply refused to respond.

On October 19, Napoleon began the retreat. He had 95,000 men left.

Winter arrived weeks later. It was brutal. But it fell on an army already shattered by logistics, disease, and starvation.

## The Lesson

Napoleon's Russian campaign failed because he tried to apply western European operational logic — fast movement, decisive battle, quick surrender — to a theater where none of those assumptions held. Russia could absorb catastrophic military defeat and simply continue fighting. The distances made decisive encirclement impossible. The scorched-earth retreat made living off the land impossible.

The winter is remembered because it's dramatic. The supply failure is forgotten because spreadsheets don't make for good paintings.

But the spreadsheets are what actually won.
